Basics · five Shu, elements, cun

How to read the atlas

The five Shu categories describe how channel qi moves between the fingers or toes and the elbow or knee. This page explains those five names, the elements they carry, the cun measurements used to locate points, and the terms that recur on every channel page.

Five Shu 五輸

Each channel has five Shu points below the elbow or knee. They are compared to the course of a stream: qi rises at the surface, gathers speed, pours, runs, and finally sinks inward at the joint. The classical images come from the Nan Jing, chapter 68, which also gives an indication for each category.

01 · JING
井
Jing-Well
jǐng xué

Qi emerges — the channel surfaces at the fingertip or toe, like water rising in a well.

Nan Jing 68Fullness below the heart

02 · YING
滎
Ying-Spring
yíng xué

Qi glides — the flow is shallow and quick, a spring trickling over the fingers or toes.

Nan Jing 68Body heat and febrile illness

03 · SHU
輸
Shu-Stream
shū xué

Qi pours — the channel widens into the wrist or ankle, where the current gathers and spreads.

Nan Jing 68Heaviness of the body; joint pain

04 · JING
經
Jing-River
jīng xué

Qi flows — the current runs strong and steady along the limb toward the elbow or knee.

Nan Jing 68Cough and dyspnea; chills and fever

05 · HE
合
He-Sea
hé xué

Qi enters inward — the channel gathers and sinks into the joint, returning to the deeper body.

Nan Jing 68Rebellious qi and diarrhea

Two categories share the pinyin jing — the Well 井 and the River 經 — so the pages keep their English names attached: Jing-Well and Jing-River. On a chart, the five points always run in this order from the extremity inward.

Elements 五行

Every Shu point carries one of the Five Elements, but the order differs by channel polarity. Yin channels run Wood → Fire → Earth → Metal → Water. Yang channels start at Metal and run Metal → Water → Wood → Fire → Earth. The two sequences interlock: a Yin channel and its paired Yang channel never give the same element to the same Shu position.

When a Shu point's element matches its channel's own element, it is the channel's horary point (本穴) — the element’s “own” point on that channel. Its position varies with the channel: the Metal Lung's horary is the River (LU8), the Wood Liver's is the Well (LR1), the Earth Stomach's is the Sea (ST36), and the Water Bladder's is the Spring (BL66). The home page lists each element's horary points across its channels.

Cun 寸

the body's own ruler

Acupuncturists locate most points with cun — a proportional measurement, not a fixed number of centimetres. A cun scales with the person, so the same “3 cun” fits a child's leg and an adult's. Ten fen make one cun, so a location “0.1 cun from the nail” is one fen.

1 cun thumb
寸

The width of the thumb at its interphalangeal joint — the crease between the two thumb bones.

3 cun four fingers
三寸

The breadth of the four fingers, index to little, held together and measured across their proximal phalanges.

0.1 cun one fen
分

One tenth of a cun. The Well points sit about this far from the corner of the nail.

B-cun (proportional cun) divides a body region into a fixed number of cun and fits that total to the person. The forearm, from cubital crease to wrist crease, is 12 cun on everyone; the lower leg, knee crease to ankle, is 16 cun. A point described as “1 cun proximal to the wrist crease” sits one twelfth of the way up the forearm — not at a fixed distance in centimetres.

F-cun (finger cun) uses the patient's own hand as the ruler: one thumb width for 1 cun, two fingers for 1.5, three for 2, four for 3. It is quick and portable, but less precise than dividing the whole region.

This atlas quotes the textbook figure for each point. The drawings show where a point sits on the limb, but they are not to scale for measuring: use the cun value, not the pixels, if you are locating a point yourself.

Worked example

LU8 Jingqu 1 cun

Divide the 12-cun forearm into twelve equal parts. LU8 sits one part proximal to the wrist crease, on the radial side of the forearm. On the Lung chart it is the River point, element Metal — the channel's horary point.

Glossary

The terms that recur in panels, locations and chart captions, grouped by what they describe.

Measurement

Cun 寸
Proportional unit used to locate points. Ten fen make one cun.
Fen 分
One tenth of a cun; 0.1 cun.
B-cun
Proportional cun — a whole body region divided into fixed cun totals, fitted to the person.
F-cun
Finger cun — measured with the patient's own thumb and fingers.

Direction & anatomy

Proximal / distal
Toward the trunk / away from it along a limb.
Radial / ulnar
Thumb side / little-finger side of the forearm and hand.
Medial / lateral
Toward the body's midline / away from it.
Palmar / dorsal
Palm side / back side of the hand.
Crease
The skin fold at a joint — wrist, elbow, knee — used as a landmark.

Points & channels

Channel 經脈
A pathway of points along the body; the atlas covers the twelve primary channels.
Yin / Yang
Paired polarities. Every Yin channel pairs with a Yang channel — Lung with Large Intestine, Spleen with Stomach.
Shu point
One of the five points between the fingers or toes and the elbow or knee.
Yuan-Source 原
Where a channel's source qi surfaces. On Yin channels it is usually the Stream point — LU9, SP3, HT7, PC7, KI3, LR3.
Luo-Connecting 絡
Where a channel connects to its paired channel. Never one of the five Shu points.
Xi-Cleft 郄
Where qi and blood gather more deeply; used for acute conditions. Never one of the five Shu points.
Horary point 本穴
The Shu point whose element matches its channel's own element — LU8 on the Metal Lung.

Reading a channel page

Every channel chart works the same way.

  1. Pick a point. Click a dot on the drawing, its text label, or one of the five chips under the panel. The point brightens and the panel fills with its details.

  2. Read the panel. You get the code, Shu category, element, textbook location and — where the source gives one — a cun distance from a named landmark.

  3. Link straight to it. A code after the address opens that point, e.g. lung-meridian-chart.html#LU8. The Points index links to every Shu point this way.

Shu Atlas is a study reference for the classical five Shu points. It is not a clinical manual: locations are simplified textbook summaries, and needling depth, technique and safety are outside its scope. Quotations of the Nan Jing are paraphrased indications, not treatment advice.
